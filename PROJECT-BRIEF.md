# Museum of You

## Project stage

Concept definition and early UX design. No user research has been conducted. Audience, needs, and proposed behavior below are working hypotheses, not findings.

## Product idea

A personal digital museum where people gather meaningful photos, screenshots, recordings, and images of physical keepsakes into small exhibitions about chapters of their lives.

The first experience: make a tiny exhibition with three objects, optionally add context, and save it somewhere you can return to.

## Confirmed direction

- Three objects provide a useful starting structure.
- Each object can have an optional note or caption. These are not Instagram-style Stories or temporary posts.
- Exhibitions persist, and people can return to them.
- The product should feel fun, distinctive, beautiful, personal, and easy to use.

## Working audience

People who already save sentimental material and want to gather a few meaningful pieces around a chapter of life. A recent move, friendship, trip, or phase gives them a concrete starting point.

This is a behavioral audience hypothesis. Age, demographics, and demand remain unvalidated.

## Problem hypothesis

People save meaningful material across apps and physical spaces, but the context can fade and related memories can be difficult to revisit together.

## Proposed user need

“Help me bring a few things from this chapter together, remember why they mattered, and come back to them without making a whole scrapbook.”

## Core journey

Create a private exhibition about one chapter, then reopen it from a personal museum.

1. Start an exhibition.
2. Give the chapter a name, with an example if inspiration is needed.
3. Add three objects from existing files: photos, screenshots, audio, or photographs of physical keepsakes.
4. Optionally give each object a title and a short note.
5. Preview an automatically arranged exhibition and reorder objects if desired.
6. Save it privately.
7. Find it in the personal museum, reopen it, and view or edit its objects.

Three objects is the initial target, not a requirement that blocks saving. Allow an unfinished exhibition to be saved so someone can return without losing work. Whether completed exhibitions should support more objects is an open design question.

## Example exhibition

**My first apartment**

- A photo of the keys: “I picked these up two days before starting my new job.”
- A chipped mug: “The only mug I owned for the first month.”
- A recording of the street: “You could hear this market from my bedroom every Sunday.”

All example content is fictional and illustrates the concept; it is not research evidence.

## First design scope

Include a personal museum, exhibition creation, object upload, optional notes, simple ordering, private saving, revisiting, and editing. Include clear feedback for saving and failed uploads. Audio must have visible playback controls and must not autoplay.

Explore sharing after the private create-and-return journey works. Public profiles, feeds, likes, collaboration, elaborate room building, and advanced layout tools are outside the first design scope.

## Design principles to test

- Start with a chapter, not an empty layout editor.
- Make adding an object useful even without writing a note.
- Provide a pleasing default arrangement before asking people to curate.
- Make saving and privacy status visible.
- Give returning to an exhibition as much care as creating one.
- Preserve readability, keyboard access, and reduced-motion preferences as visual expression develops.

## Open questions

- Does gathering three objects feel manageable and worthwhile?
- Do people understand “object” across photos, audio, and physical keepsakes?
- Do optional prompts help people add context without creating pressure?
- Can people distinguish an unfinished draft from a saved exhibition?
- Can people find and reopen an exhibition without guidance?
- What would prompt a later visit? A prototype test can explore expectations, but actual return behavior requires observation over time.

## Portfolio integrity

Describe this stage as hypothesis-led concept development. Do not present the working audience as a research-backed persona, fictional examples as interview quotes, or proposed outcomes as measured results.

The next evidence-producing step is a low-fidelity prototype evaluation. Record actual observations separately from design assumptions.
